What Is DRT in India? Complete Guide to Debt Recovery Tribunals, Filing, Jurisdiction and Appeals

If you have searched for “What is DRT?”, “DRT full form”, “how DRT cases work” or “how to file a case in DRT”, this guide explains the basic legal framework in plain language.

What is DRT?

DRT stands for Debts Recovery Tribunal. The statutory framework for DRTs is principally found in the Recovery of Debts and Bankruptcy Act, 1993, often referred to as the RDB Act. The Act provides for tribunals dealing with recovery of debts due to banks and financial institutions and also contains provisions relating to the appellate tribunal and recovery machinery. India Code identifies the RDB Act as Act No. 51 of 1993 and describes its object as providing for tribunals for expeditious adjudication and recovery of debts due to banks and financial institutions. Official India Code text.

A DRT is therefore not simply another civil court. It is a specialised statutory tribunal with powers and procedures created by Parliament for matters falling within its jurisdiction.

Why were DRTs created?

Banking-recovery disputes can involve substantial documentation, secured assets, guarantees, loan accounts and recovery proceedings. The RDB Act created a specialised forum with its own procedural framework. The current India Code text also records that the Act contains provisions for applications under section 19 and appeals to an Appellate Tribunal.

Who may be involved in a DRT proceeding?

A typical recovery proceeding may involve a bank or financial institution as applicant and a borrower or other liable person as defendant. Depending on the facts, guarantors, mortgagors or other parties can also become relevant. The precise parties and relief depend on the legal basis of the proceeding and the facts of the account.

How does a bank start a DRT recovery case?

Under section 19 of the RDB Act, a bank or financial institution seeking recovery of a debt may make an application to the Tribunal having the statutory territorial jurisdiction described in the Act. The present India Code text specifies jurisdictional connecting factors including the branch or office maintaining the account in which the debt is outstanding and other statutory criteria.

That means a DRT matter normally starts with an application supported by the relevant loan, security and account documents. The exact pleading and document requirements depend on the applicable rules and the nature of the claim.

What can happen during a DRT proceeding?

The Tribunal can deal with pleadings, evidence and interim applications within the statutory framework. The RDB Act contains provisions dealing with written statements, set-off and counter-claims, affidavits and interim protective measures. In appropriate circumstances, the Tribunal can also deal with attachment, receivership and other directions contemplated by the Act.

What is a Recovery Certificate?

One of the most important concepts in a DRT matter is the Recovery Certificate. Under section 19, after the Tribunal passes its final order, the Presiding Officer issues a recovery certificate for the amount of debt with interest to the Recovery Officer. The RDB Act then provides a separate recovery mechanism through the Recovery Officer.

This distinction is important: an adjudication order and the subsequent execution/recovery stage are connected but are not the same procedural step.

What is the role of the Recovery Officer?

The RDB Act contains a separate chapter on recovery of debt determined by the Tribunal. Section 25 lists modes of recovery, while later provisions deal with how the Recovery Officer proceeds on receipt of the recovery certificate.

For a borrower or creditor, this means that a DRT case does not necessarily end merely because an order has been passed. The recovery stage can continue under the statutory recovery framework.

What is DRAT?

DRAT stands for Debts Recovery Appellate Tribunal. The RDB Act provides for an Appellate Tribunal to hear appeals against orders of DRTs under the Act. Under section 20 of the RDB Act, an appeal is ordinarily to be filed within 30 days from receipt of the Tribunal's order.

Section 21 provides a statutory pre-deposit requirement for a borrower appealing an order: the Act states a 50% deposit of the amount of debt due as determined by the Tribunal, with power for the Appellate Tribunal to reduce the amount for recorded reasons to not less than 25%.

DRT and SARFAESI: are they the same thing?

No. The RDB Act deals with debt-recovery applications and the DRT/DRAT framework. The SARFAESI Act, 2002 separately provides a mechanism for enforcement of security interests without the ordinary civil-suit route in the circumstances covered by that Act.

There is, however, an important connection. Section 17 of the SARFAESI Act provides a statutory remedy before the DRT against measures taken under section 13(4) by a secured creditor. This means the same DRT can be involved in both RDB Act recovery applications and SARFAESI-related challenges, but the legal routes, rights and limitation provisions are not identical.

How does SARFAESI section 17 work?

Section 17 of SARFAESI allows a person, including a borrower, aggrieved by a measure referred to in section 13(4) to approach the DRT having jurisdiction. The current India Code text states that such an application is to be made within 45 days from the date on which the measure was taken, subject to the statutory framework.

The DRT examines whether the secured creditor's measures comply with the SARFAESI Act and the rules. If the Tribunal concludes that the measure is not in accordance with the Act and rules, section 17(3) provides powers including declaring the recourse invalid and, where appropriate, restoring possession or management.

Common questions about DRT

Is DRT a court?

DRT is a statutory tribunal, not an ordinary civil court. It exercises the jurisdiction and powers given to it by the governing legislation.

Can borrowers approach DRT?

Yes. Borrowers can be parties in DRT proceedings and, under section 17 of SARFAESI, a borrower can challenge measures covered by section 13(4) before the DRT.

Can a DRT order be appealed?

Yes. The RDB Act provides an appeal to the Appellate Tribunal under section 20, subject to the statutory limitation and pre-deposit framework.

Practical takeaway

When someone says “my bank case is in DRT”, the first question should be: which statutory route is involved? A section 19 recovery application under the RDB Act is different from a section 17 application challenging SARFAESI measures. The documents, limitation period, relief and procedural posture can therefore differ.
