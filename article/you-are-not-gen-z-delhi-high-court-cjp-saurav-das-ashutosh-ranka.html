“You Are Not Gen Z”: Delhi High Court’s Exchange with CJP Leaders Saurav Das and Ashutosh Ranka

Published: 21 October 2026, 00:00:00 IST ·Updated: 21 October 2026, 17:45:00 IST
By Lex Talk Legal Editorial Desk · Original-source news report · Original source ↗

A courtroom exchange over the meaning of “Gen Z” drew attention during a Delhi High Court hearing involving Cockroach Janta Party (CJP) leaders Saurav Das and Ashutosh Ranka on 21 October 2026. The remark came while the Court was hearing their challenge to a Special Executive Magistrate’s order extending their judicial custody. The proceedings also raised questions about the appropriate legal route to challenge the custody order and the circumstances surrounding the alleged breach of a peace bond.

What happened in the courtroom?

Bar & Bench reported the exchange in a post on X. During the hearing, Justice Subramonium Prasad asked about the leaders’ ages. After counsel responded, the judge remarked: “Then you are not GenZ. What is GenZ?” The exchange was part of a broader discussion about the leaders’ association, the purpose of their activities and the Court’s concerns about public peace and tranquillity.

The remark was an oral courtroom exchange, not a separate judicial ruling defining Generation Z. The core legal issue before the Court concerned the challenge to the extension of custody—not the legal meaning of a generational label.

Why were the CJP leaders before the Delhi High Court?

Reports by Bar & Bench and LiveLaw said Das and Ranka had challenged an order of a Special Executive Magistrate dated 19 October 2026 that extended their judicial custody by two days, until 21 October. The custody proceedings arose after their alleged attempt to enter the barricaded Jantar Mantar protest site, which had been de-notified as a designated protest venue. The circumstances and legality of the custody were matters raised in the proceedings; allegations should not be treated as findings of guilt.

The petitioners’ side disputed the basis for continued custody and raised issues concerning the handling and rejection of sureties. The Court, as reported, also questioned whether the challenge should first be pursued through the available revisional remedy rather than by invoking its writ jurisdiction under Article 226 of the Constitution.

What did the High Court do with the petition?

According to Bar & Bench and LiveLaw, Justice Prasad suggested that the petitioners consider withdrawing the plea and see what happened when the matter returned before the Special Executive Magistrate, while preserving such legal remedies as might remain available. Counsel eventually withdrew the petition, and the High Court permitted the withdrawal without expressing an opinion on the merits.

This distinction matters: the reported outcome was not a final adjudication by the High Court confirming the legality of the custody extension or deciding every objection raised by the petitioners. The withdrawal left the parties’ contentions open, as reflected in the reporting on the hearing.

Why did the “Gen Z” remark attract attention?

The phrase was brief and easily shareable, but it appeared within a hearing that dealt with procedural and public-order questions. The Court’s reported observations went beyond age and included questions about the organisation’s purpose, changing protest causes and the possibility that a gathering could be joined by people beyond the organisers’ control.

These were oral observations reported from the hearing. They should be distinguished from the formal order and from any final determination in related proceedings. For a legally accurate understanding, readers should consult the detailed reports and subsequent orders rather than treating a short social-media clip or quote as the full record.

The legal context: alternative remedy and Article 226

Article 226 gives High Courts broad constitutional writ powers. In appropriate cases, however, a court may ask a litigant to use an alternative statutory remedy—such as a revision or appeal—before seeking writ relief. The existence of another remedy does not operate as an automatic rule in every case; the legal route depends on the statute, the order being challenged and the circumstances pleaded.

In this hearing, the reported discussion about revision was connected to the specific challenge to the Special Executive Magistrate’s order. It should not be read as a blanket rule that a High Court can never entertain a writ petition whenever another remedy is available.

What to take away

  • The “not Gen Z” line was an oral remark during a hearing, not a ruling on generational status.
  • The proceeding concerned a challenge to an order extending judicial custody and questions about sureties and the appropriate legal remedy.
  • The petition was withdrawn, and the High Court reportedly did not decide the merits of the custody challenge.
  • The full order and later proceedings are more important for the legal position than a short social-media excerpt.
