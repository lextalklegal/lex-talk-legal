ECI Under the Scanner: What The Indian Express Investigation Revealed About Internal Dissent

Published: 27 September 2026, 15:05:40 IST
By Lex Talk Legal Editorial Desk · Educational & informational coverage · Original source ↗

A newspaper investigation has exposed sharp divisions inside India's top election body — and reignited a months-long debate over the integrity of the country's electoral rolls.



The Report That Triggered the Storm

On September 23, 2026, The Indian Express published an investigation by journalist Ritika Chopra that pulled back the curtain on the Election Commission of India's (ECI) internal functioning. According to the report, two of the three Election Commissioners — Sukhbir Singh Sandhu and Vivek Joshi — had formally recorded objections at least 14 times over a period of ten months to decisions and orders concerning electoral rolls and the Special Intensive Revision (SIR) exercise, including four objections registered on a single day. The Indian Awaaz

The dissent reportedly wasn't confined to one issue. The commissioners' concerns spanned several aspects of electoral-roll management — the addition of new voters, deletion and restoration of names, appeals against roll decisions, changes to Form 6 (the voter-registration application), and control over the Commission's voter database. The Indian Awaaz

The Indian Express reportedly followed standard journalistic protocol before publication, sending detailed questions to the Commission and to the two commissioners named in the report — but says it received no response before going to print.

What the ECI Said in Response

Facing mounting pressure, the Commission issued a statement on September 26 aimed at addressing the fallout. Rather than settling the controversy, the statement appears to have deepened it. Critics noted that the ECI's clarification effectively confirmed several of the report's underlying claims — including that agendas and minutes of meetings were not being consistently circulated within the ECI, that records were not properly maintained, and that employees on deputation had travelled abroad without the Commission's due approval or consent. National Herald

Notably, the three commissioners' meeting pattern itself became a point of scrutiny: the commissioners reportedly did not meet even once between May and August 2026, a four-month gap, but met twice in September — once on the 9th, reportedly following two commissioners writing directly to the Cabinet Secretary to flag alleged illegal conduct within the ECI, and again on the 26th, after the Indian Express report and growing public calls for the Chief Election Commissioner's removal. National Herald

The ECI has pushed back on the framing of a "split" verdict, maintaining that internal differences of opinion are a normal part of institutional deliberation, and that its decisions over the past year were ultimately unanimous.

Political Fallout

The report landed squarely in the middle of an already charged political environment around India's electoral process, and reactions came swiftly:

  • Congress and Rahul Gandhi: The Leader of the Opposition linked the findings to his ongoing "vote chori" (vote theft) campaign, which began in August 2025 with allegations of discrepancies in the Mahadevapura constituency rolls in Karnataka. Gandhi has renewed his call for Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar to step down, framing the internal objections as vindication of his broader claims about the Commission's functioning.
  • Samajwadi Party: Akhilesh Yadav questioned whether the Commission could still be regarded as fair and impartial in light of the reported internal disagreements.
  • BJP: The party has rejected the political interpretation of the report, with leaders arguing the Election Commission is a constitutional body that shouldn't be turned into a political battleground. BJP has challenged the Opposition to bring a formal impeachment motion against the CEC in Parliament if it believes the allegations are serious enough to warrant one — a notably high bar, given the constitutional threshold for removing an Election Commissioner.

The Larger Context: SIR and the Electoral Rolls Debate

This controversy doesn't exist in isolation. It's the latest chapter in a broader dispute over the ECI's Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of electoral rolls, which has been contentious since it began in 2025:

  • In Bihar, the SIR exercise flagged roughly 65 lakh voters for potential deletion over death, migration, or duplicate registration — a move opposition parties characterized as disproportionately affecting poor and marginalised voters.
  • In West Bengal, similar concerns surfaced ahead of the 2026 Assembly election, with opposition parties alleging large-scale removal of legitimate voters, while the ECI maintained the deletions targeted duplicate, shifted, deceased, or otherwise ineligible entries.
  • The matter has also reached the courts: in Association for Democratic Reforms v. Election Commission of India, the petitioners have challenged the legality of the SIR process itself, and the court has directed the ECI to disclose the names deleted from draft rolls along with reasons for each deletion.

Why This Matters, Legally and Institutionally

For an independent constitutional authority like the ECI, internal consensus and procedural transparency aren't just administrative niceties — they go to the heart of institutional credibility. A few threads worth watching:

  1. Documentation and process: If minutes and agendas genuinely weren't being circulated as claimed, that raises questions about compliance with the ECI's own internal procedures, separate from the substance of any specific decision.
  2. The unanimity claim: The ECI's position that decisions were "unanimous" sits somewhat uneasily against reports of repeated, formally recorded dissent — a tension that hasn't yet been fully reconciled in public statements.
  3. Accountability mechanisms: The BJP's suggestion that an impeachment motion is the appropriate route underscores how limited the formal options are for addressing concerns about an Election Commissioner short of a constitutional removal process — which requires a specific, high threshold under Article 324.

It's worth stressing that much of this remains contested. The political allegations — particularly Gandhi's characterization of events as "treason" — are accusations, not established findings, and the ECI disputes the narrative of dysfunction. What's been substantiated is that formal, written objections were repeatedly raised internally and that the Commission's own statement did not fully rebut the newspaper's core claims.

This piece is based on reporting from The Indian Express and subsequent coverage as of late September 2026. As this remains a developing story, readers should check for updates before relying on it for time-sensitive purposes.
