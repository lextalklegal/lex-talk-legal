Bhagwant Mann Says Paddy Yield Reached 90 Maund per Acre; Highlights Canal Irrigation

Published: 23 October 2026, 15:00:00 IST
By Lex Talk Legal Editorial Desk · Original-source news report · Original source ↗

Punjab Chief Minister Bhagwant Mann addressed the state Assembly and highlighted paddy yields, canal irrigation and groundwater levels, according to an AAP Punjab post on X dated 23 October 2026. The post says paddy yield has reached 90 maund per acre in some context, around 18,000 cusecs of canal water were delivered to fields through moghas, groundwater levels rose by up to five metres in several areas, and 14,000 kilometres of pipelines have been laid. These figures are attributed to the party’s summary of Mann’s remarks; not all are independently verified by the post itself.

What does the AAP Punjab post say?

The post summarises four points from Mann’s Assembly address:

  • Paddy yield: The post says yield reached as much as 90 maund per acre this season.
  • Canal water: It says approximately 18,000 cusecs of canal water were carried to agricultural fields through moghas—canal outlets that feed watercourses and fields.
  • Groundwater: It says water levels rose by up to five metres in several parts of Punjab.
  • Pipeline network: It repeats the figure of 14,000 kilometres of pipelines laid to carry canal water to fields.

The original post is embedded below. It is the source for what AAP chose to highlight, while the following sections compare those points with earlier published coverage and available public data.

What has been reported about irrigation pipelines?

In September 2026, The Tribune reported that Mann said canal-water use for irrigation had increased to 88%, that 14,000 kilometres of underground pipelines had been laid, and that groundwater levels had risen by between one and five metres in several blocks. The Indian Express also reported Mann’s statement that canal-water use had increased from 22% in March 2022 to 88%, with the pipeline network extended towards tail-end villages.

Those reports confirm that the pipeline and groundwater figures have been part of Mann’s public account of the irrigation programme. They attribute the claims to the Chief Minister; the reports are not themselves a statewide technical audit of the pipeline network or a block-by-block measurement of groundwater changes.

How should the 90 maund-per-acre yield figure be read?

“Maund” is a traditional weight unit used in agricultural markets in parts of North India. For clarity, a crop-yield claim should specify the paddy variety, whether the figure is a field-level maximum or an average, the crop-cutting method, the moisture/weight basis and the period covered. The AAP post does not provide those details, so the 90 maund figure should not automatically be read as the average yield across Punjab.

On 22 October, Hindustan Times reported Punjab Agriculture Department Director Gurjeet Singh Brar as saying crop-cutting experiments showed production of 67.35 quintals per hectare, compared with 58.6 quintals per hectare in the previous season, and that a bumper harvest of around 220 lakh tonnes or more was expected. This is a reported department estimate at the state level, and may not be directly comparable with a claim about the yield achieved in particular fields or varieties. The two figures need comparable definitions and a common measurement basis before being contrasted.

What does 18,000 cusecs of canal water mean?

A cusec is a flow-rate unit—one cubic foot per second—not a total volume of water delivered. The figure in the post therefore describes the approximate rate of flow being discussed, not the total water supplied across a day or season. To evaluate the impact on farms, useful details would include the measurement period, the specific canals and moghas involved, the number of outlets operating, and how much water actually reached tail-end fields.

The post does not name those locations or provide the underlying flow records. In September, other reports of Mann’s statements referred to canal-water utilisation of 88% and the 14,000-kilometre pipeline network. Those are related parts of the irrigation effort, but they do not independently validate the exact 18,000-cusec figure quoted in the October 23 post.

Groundwater: what evidence would confirm a five-metre rise?

Groundwater levels can vary significantly between districts, blocks, monitoring wells and seasons. A reported rise of up to five metres in “several areas” is not the same as a five-metre rise across all of Punjab. Verification requires data from identified monitoring locations, a clear start and end date, and a comparison made at similar seasonal points so that seasonal recharge and rainfall are accounted for.

The Central Ground Water Board publishes monitoring data and state assessments. Its groundwater-level monitoring page provides access to time-series data, and its Punjab publications include groundwater-resource and groundwater-level material. These are useful reference points for examining any claim about a change at specific sites; a statewide conclusion should not be drawn from a single maximum or a few selected areas.

Why these figures matter for farmers

Canal irrigation can reduce dependence on tube wells where water is reliably delivered, while pipelines may help move water towards fields located farther from canals. But assessing the programme’s results also requires information about the proportion of agricultural land served, continuity of supply, maintenance, water quality, crop patterns and the condition of groundwater aquifers. Yield figures similarly need to be assessed alongside procurement, input costs and crop-cutting data.

For a clearer picture, readers should look for the detailed Assembly statement, irrigation department records for flow and pipeline completion, and crop-cutting results published by the Agriculture Department. Those records would help distinguish announced infrastructure and selected examples from independently measured statewide outcomes.
