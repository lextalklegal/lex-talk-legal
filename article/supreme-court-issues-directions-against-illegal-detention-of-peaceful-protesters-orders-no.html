Supreme Court Issues Directions Against Illegal Detention of Peaceful Protesters; Orders Normal Metro, Rail and Internet Services

Published: 11 October 2026, 13:17:21 IST
By Lex Talk Legal Editorial Desk · Educational & informational coverage · Original source ↗

 


New Delhi | October 11, 2026

The Supreme Court of India on Sunday issued directions concerning the detention of peaceful protesters and restrictions on public transport, internet services and movement in Delhi, following the police action ahead of the Cockroach Janta Party’s (CJP) proposed demonstration at Jantar Mantar.

The Court directed authorities to ensure that Metro and railway services operate normally, internet facilities remain undisturbed in accordance with law, and people can move freely subject to applicable traffic regulations. It also emphasised that people participating in peaceful, unarmed assemblies must not be detained except in accordance with the procedure established by law.

The directions came during an urgent Sunday hearing involving applications alleging violations of the Court’s earlier order on public transport restrictions and the detention of protesters on October 10.

Source: The Indian Express — Supreme Court orders lifting Delhi Metro curbs

What Did the Supreme Court Direct?

A Bench comprising Justices Joymalya Bagchi and V. Mohana addressed several issues arising from the restrictions imposed ahead of the proposed CJP protest.

According to the directions reported by The Indian Express, the key measures include:

  • No unlawful detention: Peaceful, unarmed persons must not be detained except in accordance with the procedure established by law, particularly beyond areas covered by prohibitory restrictions under Section 163 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023.

  • Normal Metro and rail services: Public transport must function normally unless restrictions are required on legitimate public-order grounds.

  • Internet facilities: Internet services should remain undisturbed, subject to applicable law and the authorities’ lawful powers.

  • Free movement and traffic: Authorities must permit normal public movement, subject to traffic regulations, and review barricades in light of genuine security and traffic requirements.

  • Access to courts: The Bench directed the removal of boards restricting access to the Supreme Court or other courts, where applicable, to ensure that such restrictions do not create apprehension about access to justice.

The directions seek to limit unnecessary disruption to ordinary public life while recognising that authorities retain lawful powers to address genuine public-order concerns.

Source: The Indian Express — Report on the Supreme Court’s directions

Section 163 BNSS and the Right to Peaceful Assembly

Section 163 of the BNSS empowers designated executive magistrates to issue urgent orders in cases of nuisance or apprehended danger, subject to the statutory requirements. Such orders may restrict certain activities or assemblies where the legal conditions for their issuance are satisfied.

However, the existence of a prohibitory order does not remove the requirement that police action must have a lawful basis and comply with applicable safeguards.

The Supreme Court’s reported directions draw attention to the distinction between enforcing a valid public-order restriction and detaining individuals without following the procedure prescribed by law.

The Court’s directions concerning boards restricting access to judicial establishments are also significant. Public authorities must ensure that security arrangements do not unnecessarily interfere with people seeking access to courts or create uncertainty about whether court premises remain accessible.

Court Examines Alleged Violation of Its Earlier Order

The hearing followed applications alleging that restrictions imposed on October 10 had violated the Supreme Court’s earlier directions concerning public transportation.

According to The Times of India, the applicants alleged that Metro and railway operations had been disrupted and that several individuals had been subjected to undue harassment and detention, including outside the area covered by the prohibitory order.

The Bench issued notice to the Centre on a plea seeking contempt proceedings against the authorities concerned and asked for a response by October 13, 2026.

The proceedings also involved a habeas corpus petition filed by senior advocate Menaka Guruswamy challenging preventive detention during the demonstrations.

The allegations of contempt and unlawful detention remain subject to judicial consideration. Issuing notice on a contempt plea does not, by itself, amount to a final finding that contempt has been committed.

Sources: The Times of India — Supreme Court directions after Delhi protest crackdown | The New Indian Express — Supreme Court’s special hearing on protest detentions

Constitutional Perspective: Balancing Public Order and Fundamental Rights

The development brings the constitutional protection of peaceful assembly and freedom of expression into focus.

Article 19(1)(a) of the Constitution protects freedom of speech and expression, while Article 19(1)(b) guarantees the right to assemble peaceably and without arms. Both rights are subject to constitutionally permitted reasonable restrictions, including those relating to public order.

The right to protest is not an unrestricted right to assemble at any place without complying with applicable legal requirements. At the same time, restrictions imposed by public authorities must operate within the framework of the Constitution and the law.

The Supreme Court’s directions underline the importance of proportionality in public-order arrangements: measures intended to prevent disorder should not unnecessarily disrupt essential transport, communications, access to justice or the ordinary movement of citizens.

The legality of an individual detention will still depend on the facts, the authority invoked and compliance with the prescribed legal procedure.

Legal reference: Constitution of India — Legislative Department, Government of India

What Happens Next?

The Centre has been asked to respond to the contempt plea by October 13, 2026. The further proceedings are expected to address the allegations concerning the implementation of the earlier directions, restrictions on transport and the detention of protesters.

The matter remains important beyond the immediate demonstration because it concerns how public-order powers are exercised when citizens seek to organise peaceful assemblies.

For now, the Supreme Court’s reported directions emphasise three central requirements: public services should not be unnecessarily disrupted, peaceful individuals must not be detained outside the law, and access to justice must remain protected.
