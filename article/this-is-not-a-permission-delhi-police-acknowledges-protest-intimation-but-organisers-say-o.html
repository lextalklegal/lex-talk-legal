This Is Not a Permission": Delhi Police Acknowledges Protest Intimation, but Organisers Say October 2 Protest Will Go Ahead

Lex Talk Legal · Educational & informational coverage · Original source

 


New Delhi | 29 September 2026

A fresh flashpoint has emerged in Delhi's political and civil-society landscape. A protest is planned at Jantar Mantar on 2 October (Gandhi Jayanti), demanding the resignation of Chief Election Commissioner (CEC) Gyanesh Kumar. According to The Hindu's headline, Delhi Police acknowledged the organisers' intimation letter but made clear that it is "not a permission." The delegation, led by AISA president Neha Bora, says the protest will go ahead regardless.

What Happened at Parliament Street Police Station?

On Monday, 28 September, a delegation went to the Parliament Street police station to submit an intimation for the 2 October protest. It included Neha Bora, Yogendra Yadav, senior advocate Prashant Bhushan, Nikhil Dey, Nandita Narain and RJD MP Manoj Jha. In a joint statement, the organisers said the protest is being held "in defence of India's democratic integrity" and invited concerned citizens to join.

One part of the statement stood out. The group said it had followed due procedure and that it is now up to Delhi Police to follow natural justice and respect the people's will.

Intimation vs Permission: Why the Difference Matters

This is the heart of the story. For organisers, an intimation is a procedural step: "We are informing you, and the protest will take place." For the police, permission is a formal approval, and it has not been granted so far. The "not a permission" line reflects the two sides' very different positions.

The background matters too. Delhi Police have earlier cited prohibitory orders under Section 163 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), which replaced Section 144 of the CrPC, as being in force in New Delhi district. These orders bar assemblies of five or more people, so almost any protest at Jantar Mantar quickly runs into the question of permission.

The 25 September Detentions: Context

The intimation didn't come out of nowhere. Three days earlier, on 25 September, Delhi Police detained more than 60 protesters during an AISA-led demonstration at Jantar Mantar. National Herald's report names Yadav, Bhushan and Neha among those detained, while other reports also mention CPI(ML) general secretary Dipankar Bhattacharya. Neha Bora was reportedly detained after a heated exchange with the police.

According to The Tribune, Jantar Mantar was heavily barricaded that day, effectively turning the protest zone into a security cordon, and an NSUI march towards the Election Commission office was stopped. Police said the demonstration lacked the required permission. This time, the organisers have submitted an intimation in advance to avoid that argument.

The Demand and the Larger Issue

The immediate trigger is the demand for Gyanesh Kumar's resignation, but The Tribune's analysis suggests the confrontation goes beyond one office-holder. The street protest is testing how much space citizens, civil-society groups and political organisations have to challenge an electoral process whose legality and decision-making are themselves under public scrutiny. At the centre of the dispute is the Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of electoral rolls.

It is worth remembering that whether the allegations against the CEC hold up is a matter for the competent authorities. For now, these are claims and demands by protesters and opposition groups, not adjudicated findings.

Other Groups Are Also on the Streets

  • The Indian Youth Congress has been protesting since 25 September, seeking Kumar's resignation, greater transparency and a probe into electoral-roll decisions.
  • The Cockroach Janta Party (CJP) issued an ultimatum to Kumar, and its founder Abhijeet Dipke said a nationwide protest would begin from Mumbai on 2 October if he did not step down.

So 2 October could see activity not just in Delhi but in other cities as well.

What to Watch Next

  1. Delhi Police's final stand: Will the protest be allowed at Jantar Mantar on 2 October, or will there be a repeat of the barricading and detentions seen on 25 September?
  2. The role of Section 163: How prohibitory orders are used is likely to fuel legal and political debate.
  3. The Election Commission's response: So far, there has been no sign of any resignation. Any official response will be closely watched.
  4. The symbolism: A satyagraha-style protest on Gandhi Jayanti carries a strong political message in itself.

Conclusion

On one side are organisers who say they followed procedure and will protest. On the other is a police force signalling that receiving an intimation does not amount to granting permission. What happens at Jantar Mantar on 2 October will be more than a single protest. It will test where India draws the line between the right to protest and the demands of law and order.
