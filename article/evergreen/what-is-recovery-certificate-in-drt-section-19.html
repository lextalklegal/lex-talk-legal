What Is a Recovery Certificate in DRT? Meaning, Section 19 and Recovery Officer Explained

What Is a Recovery Certificate in DRT? Meaning, Section 19 and Recovery Officer Explained

The phrase “Recovery Certificate” appears frequently in DRT proceedings, but many readers are unsure what it means. This guide explains the certificate, the DRT's final order, and the role of the Recovery Officer under the Recovery of Debts and Bankruptcy Act, 1993.

Editorial note: This is an educational overview, not legal advice. Statutory provisions, rules, notifications, judicial decisions and facts of individual matters can change or interact differently depending on the case. Readers should verify the current official text and obtain case-specific advice where required.

What is a Recovery Certificate?

Under the RDB Act, a Recovery Certificate is the statutory instrument issued by the Presiding Officer for recovery of the debt determined by the Tribunal. Section 19(22) provides that the Presiding Officer shall issue a certificate of recovery along with the final order for payment of the debt with interest to the Recovery Officer for recovery of the amount specified in the certificate.

Why does a Recovery Certificate matter?

A DRT adjudication and the subsequent recovery stage are not necessarily the same thing. Once the Tribunal determines the amount payable, the Recovery Certificate provides the basis for the Recovery Officer to proceed with recovery under the recovery chapter of the Act.

What happens before the Recovery Certificate?

The bank or financial institution files the application under section 19. The defendant gets an opportunity to contest the claim according to the statutory procedure. The Tribunal deals with pleadings, documents, evidence, set-off and counter-claim where applicable. After hearing the parties, the Tribunal can pass an interim or final order.

The RDB Act states that the Tribunal may pass its order within the statutory framework and provides for a Recovery Certificate along with the final order.

What happens after the certificate is issued?

The Recovery Officer proceeds to recover the amount specified in the certificate. Chapter V of the RDB Act deals with recovery of debt determined by the Tribunal. Section 25 identifies statutory modes of recovery, and sections 26 onward provide additional machinery.

Depending on the matter and the statutory conditions, recovery may involve attachment, sale or other modes available under the recovery framework.

Is a Recovery Certificate the same as a court decree?

The statutory position is nuanced. Section 19(22A) states that a Recovery Certificate issued by the Presiding Officer under section 19(22) is deemed to be a decree or order of the Court for specified purposes, including proceedings referred to in that subsection. It should therefore not be described as identical to every civil-court decree for every legal purpose.

Can a Recovery Certificate be challenged?

Orders of the DRT can be appealed to the Appellate Tribunal under section 20 of the RDB Act, subject to the statutory requirements. The RDB Act provides a 30-day period from receipt of the order and a pre-deposit regime under section 21.

Separately, the Act contains provisions dealing with appeals against certain orders of the Recovery Officer. The remedy depends on what exact order has been passed and at what stage the matter stands.

What should a borrower check after receiving a Recovery Certificate?

  • Verify the amount stated in the Tribunal's final order and certificate.
  • Check whether an appeal has been filed and whether any order affects recovery.
  • Keep copies of notices issued by the Recovery Officer.
  • Review attachment or sale notices carefully.
  • Check payment, settlement or adjustment records.
  • Obtain case-specific advice about limitation and procedural remedies.

Recovery Certificate and SARFAESI proceedings

A bank can have more than one statutory recovery route in an appropriate case, but the legal effect of a DRT Recovery Certificate and a SARFAESI enforcement measure should not be conflated. The applicable statute, security, orders and procedural history matter.
